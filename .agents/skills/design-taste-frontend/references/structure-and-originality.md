# Structure and originality

Distinctive work starts with a brief-specific structure, not a fashionable
theme. A different palette on the same centered hero, three-card grid, social
proof strip, CTA band, and footer is not a new direction.

## Choose the macrostructure first

Describe the page in structural terms before selecting colors or effects. For
example: editorial index, product demonstration, split narrative, annotated
artifact, comparison-led argument, guided sequence, dense utility surface, or
image-led campaign. Select the shape that best carries the content, audience,
and conversion path.

When the brief is open, compare three or four variants that differ in reading
order, hierarchy, density, and interaction model. Do not count color, radius,
or typeface swaps as separate variants.

If the project has a `DESIGN.md` or mature incumbent system, keep its palette,
type, spacing, and component vocabulary consistent. Structural originality
must strengthen the shared product, not make every route look unrelated.

## Study references without copying them

Extract a reference's design DNA:

- macrostructure and reading path;
- dominant alignment and whitespace strategy;
- type roles rather than merely font names;
- color anchor and semantic distribution;
- surface, border, radius, and elevation rules;
- interaction rhythm and motion material.

Write what the reference solves and what conflicts with the current brief.
Rebuild the user's content with the compatible principles; never reproduce a
brand's proprietary assets, recognizable composition, or paid template.

## Structural slop review

Flag these when they are defaults rather than deliberate choices:

- the same hero-to-feature-grid-to-CTA rhythm across unrelated briefs;
- three equal cards for content with unequal importance;
- bento grids used to avoid deciding the actual hierarchy;
- icon tiles, decorative badges, fake telemetry, and version stamps that add no
  meaning;
- gradient text, glass, glow, grain, or blur standing in for art direction;
- centered alignment everywhere, or asymmetry with no reading-order purpose;
- multiple visual materials competing in the same viewport;
- motion on every section instead of one authored interaction language.

These are diagnostic signals, not universal bans. A familiar structure is
often right for a product task; use it confidently when usability or category
expectation supports it. Originality is the fit between content and form, not
novelty for its own sake.

## Pre-emit critique

Before delivery, ask:

1. Could this structure belong to an unrelated brief with only the nouns and
   colors replaced?
2. Is the hierarchy evident before decoration is considered?
3. Does every section advance the user's understanding or decision?
4. Is the implementation precise enough that restraint reads as confidence?
5. What can be removed without losing meaning, task support, or brand signal?

Sources that informed this reference include Hallmark's macrostructure and
anti-slop approach and the taste-skill family. Treat their catalogs as prompts
for judgment, not themes to install wholesale.
